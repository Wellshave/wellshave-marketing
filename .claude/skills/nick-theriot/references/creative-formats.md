# Creative Formats

Which formats are taking spend, and what makes each one work. The overriding principle:

> **Creative diversity is the trend.** Do not rely on one style. Accounts that ride a single format (only AI
> UGC, only native ads) hit a ceiling and cannot explain why. Have the *intention* to test new styles so the
> account keeps getting a fresh look.

You do not need winners in every category. You need variety in what you try.

**Why diversity actually works** (and it is not an algorithm story): **different consumers prefer different
creative types.** The format is part of the message. A worked example he gives: a client targeting a 60+
audience ran only AI UGC video and struggled with very high cost-per-link-click. That demographic came up on
print, so native long-form text ads read as familiar to them in a way short-form AI video did not. Switching
format dropped cost-per-link-click dramatically and lowered both CPM and cost-per-purchase.

So when a creative underperforms with a specific demographic, ask what media that group is *used to
consuming*, and test that shape. Format diversity unlocks new groups of people, and it also lets you reach
your existing ideal audience more effectively.

## The formats currently taking spend

**B-roll + music + captions.** Several clips of footage, no dialogue, a trending sound, and captions carrying
the message. Roughly 20–30 seconds. Show the desired state or the problem in the hook. This is the top
spending format across the majority of his client accounts.

**B-roll + AI voiceover + captions.** Same construction, but a scripted voiceover tells a story or works
through a problem instead of music. The B-roll can itself be AI-generated. Maximum control over the message.

**Animation / "Pixar-style" AI video.** Cartoon characters, either storytelling or set to an AI song. Viewers
plainly know it is animation and it works anyway. He calls this the one AI format consistently dominating
across accounts.

**Standard UGC.** Ship the product to a creator, they talk about it on camera. Still a top spending format
after producing hundreds of AI UGC videos. AI has not replaced it.

**Founder / expert UGC.** A founder, or anyone with genuine depth on the product, talking to camera about
mechanism, research and data. Works especially well in **large, sophisticated markets** where the argument has
to be won on substance. He cites a brand scaled to ~$1M/month whose best creatives consistently came from an
in-house operations person making research-led green-screen videos, beating multiple outside creative
agencies. Poor fit for small or unsophisticated markets.

**Native ads (long-form primary text + image).** Roughly 2,000–2,500 characters of primary text with a simple
image. Works notably better run through separate purpose-built pages rather than the main brand page.

**Statics.** Everything from most-aware offer statics through straightforward product-benefit statics to
unaware, native-looking statics. Still working, though generally taking less spend than B-roll video in his
accounts. See `static-ads.md`.

**Whitelisting / partnership ads.** Running ads through an influencer's own profile. He reports these
dominate for the clients who do them, and that the main obstacle is willingness to spend up front. One ad set
per creator, inside the main CBO.

**Explainer-on-a-tablet.** A person sitting in front of a tablet explaining something in deliberately
stripped-down, staccato language, with simple images. Emerging and trending. Structure it as a bridge: here is
where you are, here is where you want to be, here is the path, and here is where the product sits on it.

**Street interviews / public proof.** For products where the benefit cannot be transmitted through a screen
(smell, taste, comfort), bring in strangers to react. Use visibly different people so it reads as real.

## Worked breakdowns of exemplar ads

These are the ads he singles out and the reason each works. Use them as structures to rebuild for a different
product, never as ads to copy directly.

### The unaware / stage-5 opener
Open with a question that has nothing to do with buying anything ("who has a better life, cats or dogs?"),
compare the two, use the comparison to expose a problem, back it with a statistic, explain why every existing
solution fails, and only then introduce the product. Lower the viewer's ad defences by not being an ad yet.

Two further moves inside it worth stealing:
- **The origin story as authority**: months spent studying the problem, which earns the right to have an
  opinion about the solution.
- **The unexpected twist**: admit the first version was not good enough and had to be improved. It breaks the
  relentless pitch-pitch-pitch rhythm every other e-commerce ad has, and novelty of *structure* holds
  attention just like novelty of claim.

### The "cheaper is not worse" teardown
For a cheaper alternative to a prestige product. Physically deconstruct the expensive version, enumerate the
shared materials and components, strip out what the customer is really paying for (brand, markup, heritage),
then reveal both prices side by side. High production values are doing the persuasive work: the lighting,
close-ups and slow motion are what make the claim credible. This is a case where hiring a real videographer
is the needle-mover and AI cannot substitute.

### The belief problem
When the claim cannot be verified through a screen, you must import belief from outside. The fragrance
example: a blind test with several visibly different strangers in public, who pick the cheap alternative and
then explain why. Their explanation is what converts, not the reveal.

### The diagnosis ad
List a run of oddly specific symptoms the viewer recognises in themselves, assert they share a single deeper
cause, then introduce the mechanism that addresses it. Add urgency by tying it to a deadline the viewer
already feels. Often paired with a prop that holds attention through the list.

### Price-led ads
Two opposite versions, both valid:
- **Price as the USP**: lay out the comparison explicitly, their price versus yours, line by line.
- **Price justification**: when the market says you are too expensive, answer it head-on by showing what you
  added or improved. Expensive usually means under-justified, not over-priced.

### Authority / likeness
Put a figure the tribe already trusts in the ad, and cut between them and real-world footage of the tribe's
own environment. The cutting is what sustains attention; the environment is what signals "this is for you".

## Choosing the format

Pick on three things, in order:

1. **Where is the proof?** If the proof is visual, use B-roll or demonstration. If it is social, use UGC,
   street interviews or testimonials. If it is intellectual, use founder/expert. If it is unshowable, import
   belief from third parties.
2. **What does the awareness stage allow?** Unaware and stage-5 need story-led, native-feeling formats.
   Most-aware needs a static with an offer.
3. **What does the account not already have?** Diversity is a goal in itself.

## Production notes

- **Frankenstein ads are normal.** A single ad routinely combines a few UGC clips, some AI-generated footage
  and existing library assets. Do not treat formats as pure categories.
- **Camera angle, lighting, music and tone are not garnish**; they materially drive watch time and perceived
  value. Specify them in the script.
- **Match polish to market scepticism**: high production for perceived-value and price-comparison plays, raw
  and unedited for markets that have been burned repeatedly.
- **Props are cheap attention.** An unusual object in frame buys you the first seconds. Avoid anything the
  platform restricts.
