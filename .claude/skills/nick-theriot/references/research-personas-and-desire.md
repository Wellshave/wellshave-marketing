# Research, Personas & Desire

The foundation. Theriot's creative power comes from research, not inspiration. This file covers the three
questions that decide whether a creative can scale at all: **who is it for**, **what do they want**, and
**do they want it badly enough right now**.

## 1. Give a tribe a brand

The single most repeated positioning idea in his recent teaching:

> **Don't sell to a market. Give a tribe a brand.**
> The more accurately the customer sees themselves reflected in the brand, the less it feels like something
> being *sold to them*, and the more it feels like something *created for them*.

A broad brand asks the customer to find themselves inside it. An avatar-built brand puts them at the centre.
The pattern he points to repeatedly:

| Generic positioning | Tribe positioning |
|---|---|
| Athletes (Nike) | Lifters (Gymshark) |
| Women who want vitamins | Black women specifically |
| Women who want a bag | Women who crochet and knit |
| Anyone who wants fresh breath | Cigar smokers hiding cigar breath |
| Dog owners | Husky owners, corgi owners |

**The trade being made is TAM for dominance.** A tribe brand plucks a slice off the giant's total market and
owns it completely. You get less reach but a far higher share of the reach you get: better conversion rate,
better relevance, cheaper clicks, and natural upsell paths into more products for the same tribe.

Real results he reports from exactly this move: a breath spray repositioned from college students to cigar
smokers went from roughly $27k/month in sales to roughly $472k/month, and profit from ~$30k to ~$90k+/month.
A handbag repositioned from "women" to "women who crochet" roughly halved cost-per-purchase (~$73 → ~$35) at
the same daily spend.

**Crucially, you often do not need better creative.** You need the *same* proven creative rebuilt for your
tribe: same structure, same USPs, same script beats, with the tribe's people, settings and details on screen.

## 2. Finding the avatar: the review check-mark method

This is his concrete, deliberately manual process. Do it by hand; do not outsource it to a model that
hand-waves an answer.

1. **Start from a product that already sells.** Do not hunt for an undiscovered product. Find products
   actively scaling (he uses TrendTrack: filter for rising stores, e.g. 100k–2M monthly visits, 10–25%+
   month-over-month growth over the last six months, target country, Meta pixel present). The strategy is not
   a new product, it is a new *tribe* for a proven product.
2. **Mine the reviews for classifications of people.** Go through the brand's website reviews, plus TikTok,
   YouTube and Reddit. You are looking for people self-identifying: a breed, a hobby, a profession, a
   life-stage, an identity.
3. **Tally them.** Keep a literal list and put a check next to each classification every time it appears.
   Frequency is the signal. (Example shape: "yoga girlies" 52 checks vs "gym girlies" 1 check.)
4. **Check the lane is open.** Take the top one or two and verify no existing brand is built around that
   avatar for this product: search similar shops, and search "[avatar] + [product]" directly. Nobody there is
   a green flag.
5. **Validate the TAM** (see next section).
6. **Build brand, creatives and page around that tribe.** Not just the ads: the brand name, the product page,
   the reviews shown, the people shown. Congruence across all three is what makes it work.
7. **If avatar #1 fails, run the whole play again for avatar #2.** He gives a product two, sometimes three
   serious avatar attempts before abandoning it, usually on a ~30-day cycle per attempt.

## 3. The TAM guardrails

Niching down is the lever, but he is emphatic that going too niche is the most common way people ruin it.

- **Do not stack qualifiers.** "Cigar smokers" is the avatar. "Men who smoke two or three cigars a day, who
  are married, and play poker" is not an avatar, it is a dead end. Stay at broad, nameable groups: women who
  crochet, husky owners, cigar smokers.
- **The TikTok validation test.** Search the avatar on TikTok. You want multiple videos at 25k+ views, ideally
  100k+. That proves the tribe exists and already makes content about itself. If the top result has a few
  hundred views, the tribe is too small to scale into.
- **The doctors-vs-neurosurgeons rule.** There are ~1.2M doctors in the USA and ~400 neurosurgeons. Great
  creative aimed at a micro-market still cannot scale, because there is nobody left to show it to.
- **Rising tribes are the best tribes.** Gymshark caught bodybuilding culture as it emerged; pickleball brands
  caught pickleball. If content volume around an identity is *growing* and no brand has claimed it, that is
  the blue ocean.

## 4. Where the words come from (research sources)

You are mining the exact language, pains, desires and objections of real people:

- **Reviews** on your product and competitors' (website, Amazon).
- **Reddit and Quora** threads for literal phrasing.
- **YouTube and TikTok** content and comments in the niche.
- **Customer surveys and post-purchase surveys**; actually call customers.
- **Ad libraries** (Meta, TikTok) and ad-intelligence tools for what competitors run.

**There is no single linear process, and he says so directly.** Research is closer to how a songwriter finds
inspiration than to a checklist. Start from whichever of these four you have, then follow it outward across
sources: **the product, the avatar, the problem, or the desire.** Pick a starting point, search it on Reddit,
YouTube, TikTok and Google, read, and let ideas accumulate. On accounts he has run for years, they have worked
every angle, and even the sources that rarely produce winners still generate ideas worth carrying over.

Two pieces of advice he adds that most people skip:

- **Rotate your sources.** If you only ever use Reddit, you burn out and keep seeing the same things. Keep a
  rotating list so each research session hits somewhere you have not been recently.
- **Read between the lines.** Look for what people are *not* admitting, or are afraid to admit, and for what
  *agitates* them. What makes people upset is raw material for hooks, because it grabs attention.

**Where the thinking comes from.** He credits Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* as the single
biggest influence on his messaging methodology, and it is the source of the awareness and sophistication
grids. He also points to Gary Halbert's *Boron Letters* and Dan Kennedy. If a user wants to go deeper than
this skill, that is the reading order.

## 5. Building the persona / ICP

Feed the model everything real you have and let the persona come out of the data, not out of imagination:
the strategy-call transcript or founder interview, winning ads **and losing ads**, ad-account age/gender
breakdowns, survey exports, competitor research, and the review mining above.

A usable persona for this system carries:

- **The tribe/identity**, in the words they use for themselves.
- **What they care about right now** (their current high-priority concern, which is your point of entry).
- **The desire** they are actually buying, and the ultimate version behind it.
- **The mainstream solution they already use**, and specifically what they hate about it.
- **Their objections**, especially the unspoken ones.
- **Their proof requirements**: what would actually make this person believe you.
- **Their awareness stage and the niche's sophistication stage.**

Treat the persona as provisional. It gets sharper every cycle as the account produces learnings; that is the
point of the learnings loop in `testing-and-iteration.md`.

## 6. Choosing the desire

The same product can be sold against many desires, and the desire you choose matters more than the wording.
His method for finding candidates: search the product on Amazon, Reddit, YouTube, Instagram and TikTok, read
what people say it actually did for them, and note *who* those people were. That produces a list of desires
and avatars, which you then test.

The lever: **changing the desire changes the business.** A paint-by-numbers product sold on "anxiety and
stress" plateaued around $100k/month; repositioned to "a fun girls' night activity" (new desire, new avatar)
it scaled toward ~$1M/month. Same product.

**How to use the ultimate desire.** The desire people act on is rarely the functional one. Work down the
chain until you hit the thing they actually want:

- Functional: fresh breath after a cigar.
- Emotional: not being embarrassed, not being nagged.
- **Ultimate: your wife leans in and kisses you instead of pulling away.**

The ultimate desire is what you *show* in the hook and future-pace in the body. But note his warning about
selling *at* an emotion directly: do not ask "do you feel sad that dogs are in shelters?" Make them feel it by
showing something, then offer the solution to that feeling. **Make them feel it, then solve it.**

## 7. Emotional and desire intensity (plus seasonal relevance)

This is the piece most people miss entirely, and it is how he took an account from roughly $11k/day revenue to
~$60k/day inside two weeks: not a better ad, but the same desire targeted **when its intensity peaked**.

Desires sit on an intensity scale, from "must be nice" up to a **bleeding neck** (the person will pay almost
anything, right now). Intensity is not fixed. It fluctuates, often seasonally:

- Dry skin peaks in winter and collapses in summer.
- Weight loss peaks in January (the contrast effect after the holidays) and dips mid-summer, when people
  still feel the pain but no longer believe they can fix it before the beach trip.
- Father's Day gifting is near zero all year, spikes for two to three weeks, then falls off a cliff.
- Toys for kids: flat all year, then Q4 does the entire year's revenue.

**The rules that follow:**

1. Before committing to a desire, ask whether its intensity is high **all year** or **seasonal**.
2. If seasonal, target the peaks and lower spend through the troughs. A great ad against a low-intensity
   desire will underperform, and it is not the ad's fault.
3. **Relevance is the operative word.** Re-skin the existing offer for whatever is currently intense: rebrand
   your running offer for the holiday, the season, or the moment.
4. Set expectations honestly. A seasonal business does its money in its window; do not let anyone plan
   inventory as if January will look like Q4. He avoids taking on purely seasonal products for this reason.
5. Watch the calendar for known performance dips too: end-of-quarter (large brands dumping budget), major
   holidays, and big retail events all shift buyers away.

## 8. When the problem is not the ad

Be willing to say this, because he says it often. Signals that the bottleneck sits outside the creative:

- **No unique mechanism.** If the customer can buy the identical thing cheaper elsewhere, nothing justifies
  the price, and no amount of creative fixes it. This is his hardest disqualifier.
- **Good cost-per-link-click but bad CPA** → the creative is working, the landing page or offer is not.
- **The required ROAS is unrealistic for the category** (e.g. needing 3–4x front-end on a supplement, where
  the model normally runs near break-even front-end and makes money on retention). That is a margin and
  business-model problem.
- **Weak site, checkout, fulfilment or follow-up.**
- **No product expansion path.** Clients who scale have repeat purchases and multiple products, which raises
  LTV, which lets them accept a higher CPA and outspend competitors.
