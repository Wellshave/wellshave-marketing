# Angles & Differentiation

The strategic layer between research and execution. An **angle** is the combination of *who you talk to*,
*which desire you sell*, and *what makes you the obvious choice*. This file is the menu of ways to be
different, and how to turn that menu into an angle bank.

## Why this is the whole game

Picture your ad next to your competitors' ads. If everyone says the same thing, shows the same thing, and
sells the same product, the consumer has no basis for choosing, so they choose at random. You cannot win a
coin flip at scale.

Being different makes you the **obvious** choice. And because competitors copy whatever works, the moment your
difference is adopted, you need a new one. Good creative strategists treat this as a permanent search, not a
one-time repositioning.

## The five ways to be different

His explicit list of what has actually worked. Every angle you propose should name which one it uses.

### 1. A new mechanism / a new opportunity to get results
The strongest, because it gives a sceptical market a *reason* to believe again. Name the *how*, and pair it
with the superiority it produces.

The mindset shift he stresses: in a market where people have already failed repeatedly, stop asking whether
they have the problem. They know. They do not care that you know. **They want the opportunity that will
finally work.** So lead with the new opportunity, not the problem interrogation.

If you cannot invent a mechanism, you can often **highlight a mechanism your competitors ignore** in the same
product. Weaker (it can read as confusing rather than differentiating), but valid.

### 2. Exaggerate the claim and the visuals
Not a false claim: an escalation of specificity, scale and spectacle.

His framing device is the horror-sequel question: audiences have seen this before, so **how do we do the
version that has not been done?** If competitors film a reaction, film a bigger one. If everyone drops the
phone case off a table, drop it off a building. If the market has seen a testimonial, make the statement
startling enough that the person next to them reacts.

The compliance line matters: exaggerate the *framing and visuals*, never the claim about what the product
does or delivers. See the black-hat warning at the end of this file.

### 3. A different avatar
Same product, new tribe. Covered fully in `research-personas-and-desire.md`. The cheapest and most reliable
of the five, because it requires no product change, only new creative, copy and page built for that tribe.

### 4. A different desire / a different problem
Same product, different outcome sold. Also the move that most often un-sticks a plateaued winner. Pair it with
a new avatar and you have effectively a new business on the same SKU.

### 5. A different creative style
Being early to a format is itself a differentiator, because the audience has not built resistance to it yet.
Long-form native ads had existed for a decade, but when everyone was doing UGC video, brands that moved to
native saw them convert extremely well. Once everyone copied it, the edge evaporated and the requirement
moved back to points 1–4.

The lesson: **format novelty is real but perishable.** Keep testing new styles to keep a fresh look in the
account, and do not expect a style alone to carry you once it is crowded.

## Building an angle bank

For a given product, produce a table. Aim for 6–10 angles spanning several differentiation types and several
awareness/sophistication positions, so there is something genuinely different to test rather than ten
rewordings of one idea.

| Angle | Avatar | Desire (and the ultimate desire behind it) | Awareness | Sophistication | Differentiation type | Hook direction | Proof needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules for a good bank:

- **Every angle names its difference.** If you cannot fill the differentiation column, it is not an angle.
- **Spread the risk 80/20.** Most angles should extend what already works; a fifth should genuinely test a new
  avatar, desire, awareness stage or sophistication stage.
- **Check TAM on every avatar-based angle** before it goes on the list.
- **Check desire intensity and seasonality** before committing spend to a desire-based angle.
- **Check the lane is open.** Search the ads library for the claim; if it is crowded, move a sophistication
  stage ahead rather than joining the pile.
- **Name the proof required.** An angle you cannot substantiate visually is a script that will not convert.

## Choosing which angle to run first

Rank on four things:

1. **Openness of the lane** — is anyone already occupying it?
2. **Proof availability** — can you show it, today, with assets you have or can get?
3. **TAM and current desire intensity.**
4. **Distance from your current winner** — for the 80% bucket, close; for the 20% bucket, deliberately far.

## The compliance boundary

He is unambiguous that "black hat" advertising, deliberately lying about how a product works or what results
it delivers, is not a strategy. It produces numbers you cannot reproduce ethically, and the legal exposure is
measured against **revenue, not profit**, which can exceed everything the business ever made. Ethical
advertising may perform below a lying competitor, but it can run for years.

So: push the exaggeration into visuals, specificity, framing and spectacle. Keep the claim true and
substantiated. If a user asks for an angle that crosses this line, say so plainly and offer the strongest
compliant version instead.
