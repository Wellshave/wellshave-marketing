# Hooks

The hook is the highest-leverage element, because everything after it only happens if the hook won attention.
No hook, no watch time, no spend.

## What "the hook" actually is

Two channels, always:

- **Video:** what the viewer *sees* in the first ~3 seconds **and** what is *said/written* in the first ~3
  seconds. Both are the hook.
- **Static:** the text hook **and** the whole visual, together, in one glance.

Sometimes the visual carries most of the weight. In a watch ad he breaks down, the text hook is maybe a
quarter of the job and the visuals (lighting, slow-motion, close-ups, premium packaging) do the other
three quarters, because they make a cheap-sounding claim credible.

## The four criteria

Every hook that scales does all four. If a hook is flat, it is usually missing one.

1. **Appeals to the ideal customer.** It calls out the specific person, or shows the result they want.
2. **Intrigues curiosity.** It gives a reason to keep watching or keep reading. There must be something they
   get out of continuing.
3. **Large enough TAM.** The people it appeals to must be numerous enough to scale into. Great hooks aimed at
   micro-markets cannot spend.
4. **Says or shows something different.** If your hook matches your competitors' hooks, it will not work.

Note that #3 is a hook criterion for him, not just a targeting concern. Judge every hook on reach as well as
on cleverness.

## The 18 hook patterns currently scaling

Distilled from the patterns he reports running across ~$4M/month in spend. Each isolates one trigger. Generate
across several patterns, then pick on proof, novelty, clarity and TAM. (Examples below are illustrative
shapes, not copy to lift.)

| # | Pattern | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Result without the hated symptom** | The outcome they want, minus the thing they hate about the mainstream solution: "…lose weight without needles." Anchor on whatever everyone is currently talking about, then name its friction. |
| 2 | **Problem → solution** | Name the problem, introduce the fix. The classic direct-response beat. |
| 3 | **Failed solutions list** | "Three things [tribe] tried that did not work" — then introduce yours. |
| 4 | **David vs Goliath** | Make the problem sound so big that solving it seems impossible, then show it solved. |
| 5 | **Desired state first** | Open on a visual of the outcome people want, then reveal the solution behind it. |
| 6 | **Standard offer** | The deal, plainly. For most-aware audiences. |
| 7 | **Problem as a question** | Turn the pain into a question: "why does X have to be so bad?" |
| 8 | **News story framing** | Present the problem as a report or emerging story. Keep it compliant: no real network branding or logos. |
| 9 | **Customer testimonial open** | Start in a customer's (or their partner's) voice. |
| 10 | **Before / after** | The transformation, stated or shown. |
| 11 | **It's easy with the right thing** | "This is not hard *if* you have the right [system/tool]." |
| 12 | **Offer ending / FOMO** | Last chance, ending soon. Pairs with #6. |
| 13 | **Call out their current solution** | Name what they use now and why it fails them. |
| 14 | **Better than competitors** | Stage-4 play: what makes yours superior when everyone sells the same thing. |
| 15 | **I ditched the mainstream solution for this** | "I stopped using X, switched to this, and [result]." Plants the question "what are you using now?" |
| 16 | **Speed to result** | How fast it works: seconds, minutes. |
| 17 | **New mechanism + superiority** | Name the mechanism *and* the superior outcome it produces. |
| 18 | **Question based on the mechanism** | Ask what would happen if the actual cause were shut off. Reframes a tired market as a new opportunity. |

Additional openings he singles out as working: a **prop** used to grab attention and hold it (someone
pointing at things with an object); a **diagnosis** open that lists a run of symptoms before naming the deeper
cause; a **price USP** open; a **price justification** open ("you said it was too expensive, so we changed
this"); and **authority/likeness** (a recognisable figure or credible professional).

## The differentiation levers (what "different" can mean)

When you have the tribe and the desire, this is the menu for what you actually say differently:

- **Speed of result.**
- **The result without the thing they hate** about the current mainstream solution.
- **Price** (either cheaper, or justifying a higher price).
- **Guarantee** strength.
- **Solving the problem via a new mechanism.**
- **Exaggeration**, in the framing and the visuals, never in a false claim.

## His three-level hook creation process

He walks through this explicitly. Treat it as an escalation: each level produces better material than the one
before.

**Level 0 — set the frame.** Before writing anything, answer: *who am I writing to*, and *what do they care
about right now?* That current concern is your point of entry. Then pick which differentiation lever you are
pulling.

**Level 1 — brief the model plainly.** Give the product, the avatar, and the specific situation they are in,
then ask for hooks. Output is usually serviceable but generic.

**Level 2 — feed it inspiration.** Add your swipe file of hooks that stopped *you* scrolling, then ask again.
Quality jumps sharply. This is where the good candidates start appearing: mechanism reframes, price reframes
against what they already spend, guarantee-led lines.

**Level 3 — engineer the emotion (the emotional wheel method).** Pull up an emotion wheel, hand it over, and
ask for a hook that deliberately makes the ideal customer **feel a specific emotion**, and *that* is what makes
them keep watching. He describes intentionally provoking irritation or indignation as where some of his
best-ever ads came from.

The critical caveat, in his framing: **do not sell at the emotion, create it.** Asking "do you feel sad about
X?" fails. Showing something that makes them feel it, then offering the solution to that feeling, works.

**Then refine by volume.** Take the one you like, ask for ten more like it, and keep pulling the thread. Hooks
come from variety and volume, then judgement. Never copy-paste the model's output; use it to find the
direction, then rewrite it yourself.

## Hook research and the swipe file

Hooks are patterns you borrow and adapt, not flashes of genius.

- Save every ad that makes *you* stop scrolling. That personal swipe file is the raw material for Level 2.
- **Never copy a direct competitor.** Take structures from *other niches* and pour your context into them.
  Copying a competitor guarantees you finish below them.
- Search the problem on YouTube and Reddit and look for the bizarre, specific things people say. Those lines,
  lightly reworked, become hooks.
- Ad-intelligence tools (he uses TrendTrack) to see what is currently winning, sorted by top-performing,
  longest-active and most-duplicated ads. Longest-active and most-duplicated are the strongest signals: those
  are the ads a brand keeps paying for.

## Visual hooks

Because only the visual hook changes within a batch, this is where most of your testing happens. For one
script, design three genuinely different openings: a different setting, a different first frame, a different
person, a different demonstration, a different prop. The visual must reinforce the message, not merely look
good.

Production quality is a lever, not garnish. A well-lit, well-shot opening can carry a claim that a shaky phone
video cannot. In highly sceptical markets (hair growth, for example) the opposite holds: **raw and unedited**
outperforms, because polish reads as manufactured.

## What to output when asked for hooks

Return **6–10 options**. For each: the pattern number, the awareness and sophistication stage it fits, the
differentiation lever it pulls, and a one-line visual. Then recommend the two or three strongest on **proof,
novelty, clarity and TAM**, and say why.
