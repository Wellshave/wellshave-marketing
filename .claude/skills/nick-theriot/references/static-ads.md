# Static Image Ads

Statics run on the same psychology as video (awareness, sophistication, desire, proof, being different) but
with their own discipline: **everything must land in one glance. The whole ad is the hook.**

For a static, the hook is the text *and* the visual together. Neither alone.

## The core rule: one big idea, instantly

A static must communicate **one big idea in about half a second**. The most common failure by far is **adding
too much**: too much text, too many features, too many elements. If something does not serve the one idea,
remove it. Protect the simplicity.

## What a strong static does

- **Stands out.** Different from the feed around it, not merely well designed. If it looks like every other ad
  in the niche, it dies.
- **Shows proof.** The result or mechanism is *visible*: before/after, a clear demonstration, the product
  doing the thing, a credible screenshot, a legible comparison.
- **Lands one idea** for someone scrolling fast and not reading carefully.
- **Is not boring.** Apply the same test as video: if it feels boring to you, it is boring, and it will not
  convert.

## Headlines

- Keep the **main headline short**. People do not read long headlines on a static.
- If the message needs more, **split it**: a short punchy main headline plus a sub-headline carrying the rest.
  The first hit stays scannable; the interested reader continues.
- The headline follows hook logic: call out the avatar, show or imply the benefit, or open curiosity. A common
  strong shape is **headline plus the new mechanism named on the image itself**.

## Statics by awareness stage

He runs statics across the whole range, so pick deliberately:

- **Most aware:** the offer, plainly. Discount, urgency, ending soon.
- **Product aware:** the product and its benefit or superiority claim; comparison statics.
- **Solution aware:** the desired outcome, or your solution positioned against the alternatives.
- **Problem aware:** the problem named sharply, then the mechanism.
- **Unaware:** native-looking statics, which pair with long-form primary text. Here the image is deliberately
  plain and the persuasion lives in the copy (see `primary-text.md`).

Sophistication decides the framing: direct claim, escalated claim, new mechanism, refined mechanism, or
identity.

**The alignment check applies here too.** Copy and image must speak to the same awareness stage and the same
sophistication stage. Copy pitched at a beginner over an image pitched at an experienced buyer is a classic
static killer.

## The batch rule for statics

Same as video: **one idea = three images.** Keep the text hook identical across all three and change only the
**background or visual treatment**. That is the visual-hook test. Style variations (native, offer, comparison)
come later, once the message is proven.

## Deliverable format

```
CONCEPT: <the one big idea, one line>
Avatar: <the tribe>          Desire: <functional → ultimate>
Awareness: <stage>           Sophistication: <stage>
Differentiation: <which of the five>

Main headline: "<short>"
Sub-headline (optional): "<the rest>"
Mechanism callout (optional): "<named on the image>"

VISUAL A: <what is literally shown, and where the PROOF is visible>
VISUAL B: <same headline, different background/treatment>
VISUAL C: <same headline, different background/treatment>

Supporting element (optional): <badge, rating, arrow, callout — only if it serves the one idea>
Why it stops the scroll: <the novelty / what makes it different>
Primary text direction: <short, or long-form native — see primary-text.md>
```

Then provide **3–6 concept variations that change the angle**, not the colours: different idea, different
proof, different avatar call-out. That is where the testing leverage is.

## Production

His workflow: the creative strategist decides the desire, the message and what must be shown, pulls
inspiration from currently-scaling static ads in *other* niches, and briefs a designer. The designer builds it
with image tooling, often generating a reference image first and then the final composition.

The strategy is the scarce part. The rendering is not. Do not let tooling choices substitute for deciding what
the image has to prove.

## Static review checklist

1. One big idea, graspable in half a second?
2. Does it stand out from the feed, or blend in?
3. Is the main headline short? Is longer copy split into a sub-headline?
4. Is proof visible (result or mechanism shown), not merely claimed?
5. Do copy and image match on awareness **and** sophistication?
6. Is it overloaded? What can be removed without losing the idea?
7. Is the avatar clearly called out, or the result clearly shown?
8. Is the TAM large enough for this call-out to scale?
9. Is it boring?
