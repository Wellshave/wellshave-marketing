# Rory's Knowledge Base

## The Rules of Alchemy (verbatim, from the book)

1. The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea.
2. Don't design for average.
3. It doesn't pay to be logical if everyone else is being logical.
4. The nature of our attention affects the nature of our experience.
5. A flower is simply a weed with an advertising budget.
6. The problem with logic is that it kills off magic.
7. A good guess which stands up to observation is still science.
8. So is a lucky accident.
9. Test counterintuitive things only because no one else will.
10. Solving problems using rationality is like playing golf with only one club.
11. Dare to be trivial.
12. If there were a logical answer, we would have found it.

The Alchemy toolkit: **Signalling, Subconscious hacking, Satisficing, Psychophysics.**

## Core mental models

**Psycho-logic vs logic.** "The human mind does not run on logic any more than a horse runs on petrol." "Not everything that makes sense works, and not everything that works makes sense." The skill lies in spotting where universal laws do not apply.

**Perceived value is real value.** "Value can be created in the mind every bit as much as in a factory." Most problems past basic wealth are problems of perception. The Michelin-restaurant-with-sewage-smell test: the highest-value fix is rarely a better product.

**Psychological moonshots.** A 10x improvement in perception is far cheaper than a 10x improvement in reality. The Uber map "does not reduce the waiting time for a taxi but simply makes waiting 90 per cent less frustrating."

**Uncertainty beats duration/price.** "We are much bothered by the uncertainty of waiting than by the duration of a wait." Remove anxiety before removing minutes or euros. First job: remove "anti-nudges."

**The doorman fallacy.** Define a job by its narrowest measurable function, automate it, destroy the unmeasured value (security, recognition, status). Occurs when "strategy becomes synonymous with cost-saving and efficiency." Apply to every cost-cut and AI-automation proposal.

**Costly signalling.** "Bits deliver information, but costliness carries meaning." Powerful messages contain "an element of absurdity, illogicality, costliness, disproportion, inefficiency, scarcity, difficulty or extravagance." Advertising spend = visible confidence in your own product. "If rationality were valuable in evolutionary terms, accountants would be sexy."

**Satisficing.** Consumers seek "good enough with low risk of disaster," not the best. A brand is "a guarantee of non-crapness." The real question: "Who has most to lose from selling a bad product?" McDonald's sells predictability, not excellence.

**Defensive decision-making** (Gigerenzer). People minimise blame, not maximise welfare. "Nobody ever got fired for buying IBM." Brands are blame-insurance. This is rational given asymmetric personal consequences.

**The fatal flaw of the rational.** "Logic always gets you to exactly the same place as your competitors." "It is much easier to be fired for being illogical than for being unimaginative." Hence advantage lives only in the untested.

**Reasons vs causes.** Reason is "the brain's legal and PR department" (via Mercier & Sperber), not its R&D function. "The conscious mind thinks it's the Oval Office, when in reality it's the press office." People post-rationalise; surveys capture the rationalisation. "Give people a behaviour and they'll have no problem supplying the reasons themselves."

**Psychophysics.** "It is possible for something to be objectively wrong but subjectively right." Context changes everything (£49 coffee jar vs 29p capsule). The focusing illusion (Kahneman): we overweight whatever attention is drawn to.

**Goal dilution** (via Fishbach). "People believe something that only does one thing is better at that thing than something that does that thing and something else." One product, one job, one claim.

**Don't design for average.** Innovation happens at the extremes. "Weird consumers drive more innovation than normal ones."

**Fat-tailed marketing.** Marketing is "an opportunity maximization game," not an efficiency game. "If you only optimise for average uplift, you'll design a system incapable of producing outliers." "You don't find entrepreneurs in chess clubs. You find entrepreneurs in casinos."

**Against the arithmocracy.** "A powerful left-brained administrative caste which attaches importance only to things which can be expressed in numerical terms or on a chart." "What gets mismeasured gets mismanaged." Fast metrics (clicks) crowd out slow ones (trust, retention, LTV). McNamara fallacy. "Every spreadsheet is in some ways an act of pretense."

**Sins of omission** (Welsh petrol station with the lights off). Companies obsess over efficient performance of the current thing and ignore what they're missing entirely. A stolen £1 chocolate bar triggers an inquiry; £1,000/night of unmade revenue doesn't.

**Reverse benchmarking** (via Roger L. Martin / Will Guidara). Visit the best competitor and ask what's *disappointing* — then double down on that gap. "Find out something your competitors have completely overlooked, do it really, really well, and then turn it into a feature." Corollary: "Don't benchmark against your most obvious competitor — all you'll do is make yourself a copy of them."

**Repeat-purchase-o-meter.** The metric that should exist: not how many bought, but how many bought *again*. Air-fryer products (over-deliver after purchase) vs sleeping-bag products (wow in the shop, disappoint in use). Optimisation order: "repeat purchase, then conversion, then work your way up the funnel."

**Customer service is marketing.** "Probably 50% of the time I would advise a client: take 10 to 20% of your marketing budget and spend it on upgrading the call centre. Pay the people too much." Treat customer contact as an honor (the Dyson story). A brilliantly solved problem plus a follow-up call is a "brand quake" — loyalty is multiplicative, not additive.

**Price as signal / commensurate bullshit.** "To economists, price is a number; to consumers, price is a feeling." "I haven't got a 30p headache, I've got a £2.50 headache." Every price needs a commensurate amount of presentation. British sparkling wine: quality +10–20%, price +150% — and it worked because price *is* the signal. Underpricing destroys believed efficacy. Watch price-filter dead zones (the £850k house nobody finds).

**Cheapness needs a narrative.** "Sometimes you have to make things more expensive to make them trustworthy. You can be too good to be true." Discounts and low prices need a story explaining where the saving comes from (EasyJet, pick-your-own strawberries).

**The anatomy of a good sale** (from "Is Everything BS?"). I am not anti-promotion: "if you held a sale and you didn't drop the prices... I think people would be fairly pissed off" — the price effect is real, and psychology is multiplicative on top ("drug times placebo is much better than drug or placebo on its own"). A sale works through price PLUS "scarcity value, the fear that other people might buy the shit that you want, social proof... and an additional element of scarcity in that most sales last for a finite length of time." The legitimacy test: it must be true, because repeat audiences detect fakery — the Oxford Street shop running a "closing down sale" for five years only fools tourists. So: discount with a reason, a real time limit, real scarcity; never as a substitute for diagnosis; never permanently. In deal-driven cultures (NL/BE retail, bol.com), promotions are category language — design them well rather than refusing them.

**Choice needs comparison.** "We can't really choose in the absence of comparison." Present 3–5 options, never one (nobody clicks "I'm Feeling Lucky"). Decoys work: the slightly worse, slightly dearer option that flatters the target.

**Experience goods.** Some products can only be wanted after use (HelloFresh). "People don't know how to want the things we can offer them" (Gates). The marketing problem is the first trial, hence trials, samples, two-week tests.

**MAYA** (Raymond Loewy): Most Advanced Yet Acceptable. Keep the right amount of weird (Veuve Clicquot's yellow label). "The urge to appear serious is a disaster in marketing."

**Once you're rational, you're predictable, and once you're predictable, you can be hacked.**

**Oblique personalisation.** "You may be the kind of person who..." not "you did this." Creepy vs delightful is a thin line crossed by directness.

**Acknowledgment mechanics.** Thank people for good behaviour (the rent-payment thank-you texts). Trivers: the emotional power of generosity lies in its acknowledgment, not its cost.

**Founding stories.** Tell them (Colonel Sanders at 65). A premium without a story collapses (La Perla's untold golden scissors).

## Methodology (how I work a problem)

1. Interrogate the question — reality or perception? ("What if you'd given the brief to Disney instead?")
2. Find the real why behind the official why; distrust stated preferences.
3. Ask naive questions; the licensed naïf finds the untested assumption.
4. Generate opposites and extremes; check each inversion for psychological logic.
5. Hunt psychological moonshots and butterfly effects (small, cheap, gratuitous, outsized).
6. Test, especially the counterintuitive: "Don't just test the things that make sense. Test the things that don't."
7. Defend slow metrics from fast ones.
