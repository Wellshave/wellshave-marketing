# Standing Context: Wellshave

Read this file when the conversation concerns Wellshave or men's grooming. For other brands, skip it — but the category principles below travel well to any considered D2C purchase.

## The brand

Wellshave is Dustin's direct-to-consumer men's grooming brand (Netherlands-based, Dutch + English markets): body/groin trimmers and shaving care. Channels: Shopify store, Meta ads, Klaviyo email, bol.com. The team uses specialist skills for execution (Dylan for Wellshave copy, Eva for product photography prompts, Ogilvy ad-copy and advertorial skills); Rory is the strategic thinking partner upstream of those.

## My standing views on this category

**The real why.** Nobody buys a body trimmer for blade specifications. The real motives: confidence, hygiene anxiety, partner perception, not being embarrassed, feeling in control before situations that matter (dates, gym, summer). The official why ("precision", "skin-safe") is the alibi the customer needs. Write to the real why; supply the alibi.

**Anxiety is the conversion bottleneck, not price.** A blade near the body is a trust purchase. The buyer's question is not "what's the best trimmer?" but "who has most to lose from selling me a bad one?" Uncertainty-killers (skin-safety promises, warranty, reviews, delivery clarity, easy returns, a visible phone number) will usually outperform discounts.

**Price as signal is unusually strong here.** Believed safety and believed performance follow price (the £2.50 headache principle). Underpricing a device that touches skin actively damages trust. If anything is cheap, give the cheapness a narrative.

**Cost per entertainment hour / daily-use justification.** A premium daily-use product is cheap per use — the frame I use for watches and games consoles applies directly to a trimmer used for years.

**Never lecture the base.** The Gillette toxic-masculinity ad is my canonical category disaster: "needlessly insulting towards men." Defend the buyer (against shame, awkwardness, razor burn), never instruct or moralise at him. Humour that laughs *with* the customer about the awkwardness of the subject is the right register — the smoker-philosopher reframe, applied to grooming.

**Identity over instruction.** "Don't Mess with Texas" logic: tie the behaviour to who the customer already is (a man who takes care of himself, an optimiser, an athlete) rather than arguing he should change.

**Satisficing brand.** Wellshave should be the "guarantee of non-crapness" in a category full of anonymous drop-shipped devices — predictability, safety and service as the moat, not maximal features. Goal dilution warning: one product, one job, one claim per ad.

**Repeat purchase first.** Blades/heads, skincare refills and subscriptions are the business; the optimisation order is repeat purchase → conversion → traffic. Be an air fryer, not a sleeping bag: over-deliver after the box opens.

**Reverse-benchmark the giants.** Philips, Braun and Gillette all neglect: post-purchase human service, honest first-timer education (especially groin grooming), the anxious novice, packaging as a moment. Pick one neglected thing, do it superbly, give it a name, make it a feature.

**Things I'd want tested at Wellshave** (hypotheses, not promises):
1. Trust-led PDP ("who has most to lose") vs feature-led PDP.
2. A premium-priced bundle with commensurate presentation vs a discounted one.
3. Delivery courier choice + visible support phone number; measure repeat rate, not just conversion.
4. A slightly gratuitous in-box detail engineered to be mentioned in reviews.
5. Welcome flow as anxiety-removal sequence (milestone chunking: first trim → week two → full routine) vs cross-sell sequence.
6. Thank-you mechanics for subscribers (acknowledge renewals; never only contact people to sell).
7. An ad that opens on the inversion ("The best trimmer ad says nothing about blades") — my viral-clip formula: contrarian hook, everyday situation, mechanism revealed, one idea per ad.
8. The founding story, told properly, on the brand page and in the welcome email.

**Filter dead zones.** Check Wellshave price points against bol.com/Amazon/Google Shopping filter boundaries (the €850k-house principle at €-double-digit scale: €49/€50, €99/€100).

**Discounting in NL/BE — the local reality.** Dutch and Belgian consumers are unusually deal-oriented: Black Friday, Sinterklaas/holiday promos, bol.com's discount-driven merchandising and price filters are part of the category language, and refusing to speak it entirely is its own handicap. My position for Wellshave: promotions yes, reflexes no. Rules of thumb — (1) every discount gets a narrative the customer can repeat (launch week, seasonal, bundle logic, "first kit" trial pricing for an experience good); (2) real time limits and real scarcity only — repeat customers detect fakery; (3) protect the hero product's price integrity: discount bundles, starter kits or older models rather than the flagship, or add value (gift, upgrade, content) instead of subtracting price; (4) on bol.com, play the promo calendar as a *visibility* game (deals drive placement) while the own webshop carries the premium frame; (5) a discount that "works" is still not a diagnosis — check what problem it papered over.
