# The Evidence Layer — Hook Patterns That Currently Take Spend

My seven hook families in `scripts.md` are **mechanisms**: reasons a hook works on a human being. This file is
a different thing and should be used differently. It is a **field record** of the hook shapes currently taking
real money on Meta, distilled from a media buyer running roughly $4M a month, plus the traits his
six-figure-spending ads share.

Treat the distinction seriously, because it is the whole point of keeping both:

- **My families tell you *why* a hook works.** They generate ideas, including ideas nobody has tested.
- **This file tells you *what is currently working*.** It disciplines those ideas against observed reality.

Neither is sufficient. Mechanism without evidence produces clever ads that fail; evidence without mechanism
produces competent copies of whatever won last quarter, which is how a category ends up with a thousand
identical ads and no reason to choose between them. Generate from mechanism. **Sanity-check against evidence.
Never generate *from* the evidence list alone** — that is a recipe for the category average, and the category
average is what we are paid to escape.

## The four criteria a hook must clear

Before any hook leaves this studio it needs all four. The third one is the one creative people forget:

1. **It appeals to the specific person** — calls them out, or shows the result they want.
2. **It opens curiosity** — gives a reason to keep watching or keep reading.
3. **Its audience is large enough to scale into.** A superb hook aimed at a market of four hundred people
   cannot spend. This is a *creative* constraint, not a targeting one, and it kills more good ads than bad
   writing does.
4. **It says or shows something different.** A hook that matches the competitors' hooks does not work.

## The observed patterns, grouped by my families

Where a pattern maps onto one of my mechanisms, I have said so — that mapping is how you use this file
generatively rather than imitatively.

**Under Inversion**
- *The result without the hated thing.* Name the outcome, minus the friction of whatever everyone is currently
  talking about. The mainstream solution's most-hated attribute becomes your opening.
- *I ditched the mainstream solution for this.* Plants the question the viewer answers themselves.
- *The failed-solutions list.* Everything they tried that did not work, then yours.

**Under Gatekept mechanism**
- *New mechanism plus superiority* — the how, paired with what it produces.
- *The question built from the mechanism* — what would happen if the actual cause were shut off? Reframes an
  exhausted market as a new opportunity, which is exactly the stage-3 move.
- *Speed to result* — how fast, specifically.

**Under Anxiety named**
- *Problem as a question.*
- *The diagnosis open* — a run of oddly specific symptoms the viewer recognises in himself, then the deeper
  cause. Powerful and easy to abuse: name the fear kindly, defend the buyer, never shame him.
- *Call out their current solution* and why it is failing them.

**Under Price-as-signal**
- *Price as the USP* — the comparison stated plainly, line by line.
- *Price justification* — "you said it was too expensive, so here is what we changed." Expensive nearly always
  means **under-justified**, not over-priced.

**Under Identity / Satisficing**
- *Better than the competitors* — the stage-4 play when everyone sells the same thing.
- *Customer testimonial open.*
- *Before / after.*
- *It is easy if you have the right thing.*

**Offer-register (most-aware only)**
- *The standard offer*, and *the offer ending* — real deadline, real narrative.

**Presentation shapes, not mechanisms**
- *News-story framing* (keep it compliant: no real network branding).
- *A prop* used to hold attention through a list.
- *Desired state shown first*, then the reveal.
- *David and Goliath* — make the problem sound unsolvable, then solve it.

## The eight traits of ads that spend

Use this as the **pre-launch scorecard**, whatever the format. It comes from the ads that each spent six
figures, and it is the most useful checklist in this file.

1. **A visual hook that grabs attention** — something not seen a hundred times before, catching the *right*
   person.
2. **Built for a large audience** — the call-out, problem and desire are widely shared.
3. **Intention behind every word** — each line does a job. Nothing is filler.
4. **Built to close on this ad alone** — problem, why it matters, how it works, proof, CTA, all present.
   In reality people see several ads first; ads *built* to close single-handedly are the ones that spend.
5. **Explains the product very simply** — a confused mind says no.
6. **It is different** — visual, avatar, mechanism, or format.
7. **It is relevant right now** — tied to what the audience is feeling this week.
8. **It is not boring.**

Note how many of these are mine in other clothing: 3 is intention, 5 is goal dilution, 6 is inversion, 8 is
the thing I say about solemnity. Trait 2 is the one genuinely absent from my instincts, and it is the one that
most often explains why a beautiful ad never spends.

## Relevance and the timing of desire

Trait 7 deserves its own note, because it is the cheapest performance lever in this entire skill and it is
almost never a creative problem.

Desires are not constant. They spike and collapse — dry skin in winter, gifting in the fortnight before the
occasion, weight loss in January and never in July. A desire has an intensity, and at its peak people will pay
almost anything while at its trough the identical ad underperforms and gets blamed for it.

So before committing to an angle, ask whether its desire is *currently intense*, and if it is seasonal, aim at
the peak and re-skin the running offer for whatever is intense now. When someone shows me a script that
"stopped working", my first question is no longer about the script.

## The batch reconciliation

Our two systems disagree about what to vary, and the disagreement matters:

- **Our Notion 3x6x3**: three *verbal* hooks, one body.
- **His production rule**: one verbal hook, three *visual* openings, because each new idea is itself the
  verbal test.

Both are right about different variables. We keep 3x6x3 because the database is built on it — but each of the
three hooks must also carry a **genuinely different opening shot**, not merely a different sentence over the
same footage. Vary the door *and* how it is lit. Otherwise the visual hook, which is doing most of the work in
the first second, is silently held constant across the whole test.
