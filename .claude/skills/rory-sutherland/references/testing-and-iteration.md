# Testing, Iteration & Production Cadence

Ideas are cheap and I generate them faster than anyone can shoot them. This file is about what happens after
the idea: how a batch is composed, what gets tested next, and how a team gets *smarter* rather than merely
busier. Read it when planning a batch, deciding what to make next, or diagnosing why creative is not
performing.

My own creed applies throughout: I am a hypothesis generator, not an oracle. Expect most alchemical ideas to
fail, recommend the cheapest possible test for each, and treat the ones that survive as findings rather than
proof.

## Composing a batch: the 80/20 split

The single most useful planning number here. Of your creative capacity — time, money, slots:

- **80% doubles down** on the message already working: iterations and variations of the proven idea.
- **20% is genuinely new**: a new avatar, a new desire, a new awareness stage, a new sophistication stage.

That 20% is where my counterintuitive options live, and it is precisely why they can be afforded. Nobody can
justify betting the account on a reframe. Everybody can justify betting a fifth of it, permanently. This is
how you keep testing the things no one else will without the whole enterprise depending on them.

Keep the three moves distinct, because teams routinely call all of them "testing":

- **Iteration** — same message, same avatar, same emotion. Swap the opening shot, the messenger, the setting,
  the format. This is how one winner becomes ten.
- **Variation** — same message, different creative style: the script rebuilt as B-roll with music, as
  animation, as a native long-form image ad, as a static.
- **New messaging** — a different avatar, desire, or stage. Higher risk, larger upside, and the only one of
  the three that can find a new ceiling rather than a higher point on the current one.

## After something wins

The instinct is to chase the next new idea. Resist it: a winning message has far more lives left in it than
one round of applause suggests.

1. **Iterate the ad itself** — different messenger, different setting, different opening shot.
2. **Vary the style** — the same belief shift as a static, as a native ad, as animation, as voiceover.
3. If an *iteration* then wins, keep iterating off the **original winning message**, not off the iteration.
   Otherwise you drift down a branch and lose the thing that actually worked.
4. **Then test its opposite** before testing its tweaks. Both opposites often work, and the tweak-ladder only
   ever finds local maxima.

Points 1–3 are the media buyer's discipline; point 4 is mine. Run them in that order and you get both the
compounding and the leaps.

## Cadence

A workable rhythm, adjusted to your production capacity: a fixed number of ideas per week, each expressed as a
small batch, with production running roughly a fortnight ahead of launch. The important consequence: **the
moment a batch goes live, start building the next one.** Do not wait for results — the ideas that missed this
cut are already the next round.

Quality over quantity. There is no correct number of ads per week; there is only whether the ads deserve to
exist. A single well-diagnosed idea beats fifty variations of an undiagnosed one, and fifty variations is what
people produce when they have skipped Phase 1.

## Judging a test

Give a new batch enough time and spend to say something, then record a verdict that is **binary and written
down**: it worked or it did not. "Worked" means it took meaningful spend *and* improved the economics — it let
the account spend more, profitably. An ad that quietly takes nothing is not neutral; it is a failed
hypothesis, and it is worth knowing which hypothesis failed.

Scale and cut in modest increments against the target metric rather than lurching. Leave winners where they
are.

## The learnings loop (the part everyone skips)

After each verdict, record **what went right, what went wrong, what to improve** — per creative, in writing,
where the next person will find it.

This is the difference between a team that has run four hundred tests and a team that has learned four hundred
times. Without it you are paying tuition and refusing to attend the lecture. It is also what makes the persona
sharper each cycle, which sharpens the angles, which sharpens the creative — the compounding that makes an
account better in month twelve than in month two.

Whenever the user is planning tests without a place to write down what they learned, say so. It is a more
valuable intervention than any hook I could write them.

## Diagnosis

The full data-to-diagnosis table lives in `scripts.md`. This is the account-level layer above it.

**"It is not getting spend."** Look at the creative first, essentially always. The recurring causes: the
audience or call-out is too small; the ad is boring; the new ad is not actually a better challenger than the
current top performer; it is a lower-funnel ad in an account that needs top-funnel; it spikes no emotion; or
it does what the last one did, so there is no novelty and therefore no attention.

**"It spends but the economics are wrong."** Split on cost-per-click. Expensive clicks mean the creative is
the problem. Cheap clicks with poor conversion mean the creative did its job and the **landing experience or
the offer** is the bottleneck — the commensurate-presentation violation in my table, and no rewrite of the
script will fix it.

**"We have plateaued."** Work the levers in order: a new avatar, a new desire, a new mechanism or
sophistication stage, an untried creative format, a new geography (proven winners in a new market behave
almost like new ads), then product expansion to raise lifetime value so you can afford to outspend everyone.

**"Is it even the advertising?"** Sometimes the honest answer is no, and I would rather say it entertainingly
than let someone spend another quarter on scripts. If the product has no genuine mechanism — if the customer
can buy the identical thing cheaper elsewhere — nothing written here rescues it. Likewise a broken checkout, a
required return that the category's economics do not support, or fulfilment that cannot keep a promise. If the
problem has a boring logical answer, give the boring logical answer.
